A gas water heater is a fairly simple machine, which is part of why it lasts. A burner under the tank heats the water, exhaust rises through a flue that runs up the center of the tank, and a gas control valve on the front acts as both thermostat and safety shutoff. That valve only lets gas reach the burner while it senses a flame, through a thermocouple on standing-pilot models or a flame sensor on electronic ignition models. When something goes wrong, it is usually at one of those points. In Flint, MI, we regularly find thermocouples that no longer hold the valve open, pilots starved by a lint-clogged flame arrestor screen, gas valves that have failed internally, and flue or vent problems that let exhaust spill back into the room.

Flint Water Heater does not guess with gas appliances. On every Flint call, our technicians test the thermocouple's millivolt output, inspect the pilot and burner flame, check gas pressure at the valve, and run a draft test at the draft hood to confirm exhaust is actually leaving the house. We look inside the combustion chamber for scorching, rust, and signs of flame rollout, which tell us whether the flue is blocked or the burner is misbehaving, and we check for carbon monoxide around the heater. One rule comes before all of it: if you smell gas, do not light anything or flip switches. Leave the house, call your gas utility from outside, and then call us once it is safe.

Once we know the cause, the repair is usually simple. A new thermocouple or flame sensor, a cleaned pilot tube and burner, a cleared flame arrestor screen, or a replacement gas control valve gets most heaters running again the same day. Vent problems, like a disconnected connector or a flue pipe without enough rise, are corrected on the spot. If sediment is rumbling in the tank, we flush it, and if the anode rod is spent, we can replace it. When replacement is the smarter move, we install new gas heaters sized to your household's first-hour demand, with proper atmospheric, power vent, or direct vent venting, a drip leg on the gas supply, and the expansion tank, drain pan, and strapping your local code calls for. Symptoms That Mean It's Time to Call

Water heaters usually give plenty of warning before they fail, as long as you know what to look and listen for. These are the red flags our technicians see most often before a breakdown:

  • Any smell of gas around the appliance, which calls for leaving and phoning the gas utility
  • Loud rumbling from the bottom of the tank
  • Less hot water than the tank should deliver
  • Water pooling around the base of the heater
  • Pilot outages that keep coming back after a relight
  • A cold tank and a burner that will not come on
  • A flame that looks orange or floppy rather than steady and blue
  • Exhaust odor, rust, or moisture around the draft hood and vent pipe

What a Trained Technician Brings to the Job

Gas water heaters are among the riskiest appliances to work on without training, because the biggest hazards are invisible. A fitting that is almost tight can seep gas for days before anyone notices. A vent connector that has lost its slope, or a flue partly blocked by debris, can let carbon monoxide spill into the house every time the burner fires, with no smell to warn you. And many repairs, including gas valve and heater replacement, require a permit and inspection in most areas. Relighting a pilot by following the label is something most homeowners can do; almost everything past that deserves a technician.

Professional service gets you a correct answer and a safe appliance. We confirm thermocouple output, gas pressure, burner flame, and draft with real instruments, not trial and error. Every gas connection we make is tested for leaks before we leave, and vent connectors are secured with the right slope and clearances. When a replacement is needed, we size the new heater by first-hour rating, handle the permit where one is required, and add the expansion tank, sediment trap, drain pan, or seismic straps your home needs. Tips to Keep Your Water Heater Healthy

Most of the failures we see could have been delayed for years with basic upkeep. Here are our recommendations:

  • Clean dust from around the base so the burner gets plenty of combustion air
  • Keep working CO alarms near bedrooms and on each floor
  • Replace a spent anode rod before the tank itself starts to rust
  • Watch for a loose, rusted, or sagging vent pipe above the heater
  • Flush a few gallons from the drain valve periodically to control sediment
  • Keep the thermostat around 120 degrees to save energy and reduce scalding risk
  • Give the heater breathing room and keep flammables well away from it
